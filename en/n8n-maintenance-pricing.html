How much should you charge for n8n automation maintenance?

A transparent framework for estimating monthly hours, incidents, margin, and the limits of your support plan.

Illustrative example · October 21, 2026

Automation maintenance is more than checking whether a workflow is running. Work may include reviewing executions, diagnosing failures, updating credentials or integrations, handling platform changes, and communicating with the client.

A recurring price based only on the number of workflows can miss that variability. Estimate workload explicitly, calculate a cost-based floor, define the service boundary, and then compare your assumptions with the time you actually spend.

Transparency: every number in this guide is an illustrative calculation, not a market average or a guarantee that a client will accept the price. Adjust assumptions to your service, location, experience, and support commitments.

1. Break the workload into categories

  • Routine checks: review execution status, alerts, logs, and integration health.
  • Incidents: estimate likely incidents per month and the average time needed to diagnose and fix each one.
  • Coordination: reporting, meetings, messages, deployments, and change management.
  • Incident reserve: a stated buffer for variation. It is not a replacement for scope limits or an unlimited incident promise.

Use your own execution history wherever possible. A stable workflow may need very little attention; a critical automation dependent on several external APIs can require much more.

2. Estimate monthly hours

Hours with reserve = (routine hours + incident hours + coordination) × (1 + reserve percentage)

3. Turn cost into a price

Estimate your internal cost per hour, including the time and overhead required to provide the service. Then use the target gross margin rather than an arbitrary markup.

Labor price = estimated labor cost ÷ (1 − target gross margin)

For example, 7.5 estimated hours at an internal cost of $60/hour means $450 in labor cost. With a 40% gross margin target, the labor price is $450 ÷ 0.60 = $750. The currency is illustrative, and third-party expenses are not included in this calculation.

Margin and markup are not the same. Multiplying $450 by 1.4 gives $630, which does not produce a 40% gross margin.

The $60/hour, 40% margin, and 7.5-hour assumptions are examples used to demonstrate the formula, not a recommended market price.

4. Keep external costs and service levels explicit

  • Hosting, licenses, and API usage: bill them separately or include them deliberately; do not count the same cost twice.
  • Priority availability or SLA: price it only if you can define and meet the coverage hours, severity levels, and response targets.
  • Extra hours: set a rate and approval process before work exceeds the included hours.
  • New development: features, major migrations, and changes in business scope may require a separate quote.

5. Define the support scope

Before a client signs, document which workflows are covered, what routine work is included, how hours are tracked, support hours and channels, incident severity, response targets, exclusions, and who approves third-party spending. A monthly retainer does not automatically include 24/7 coverage.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I charge per n8n workflow?

There is no universal price. Workload, criticality, external dependencies, incident history, scope, and response-time commitments matter more than workflow count alone.

Should hosting and API usage be included?

That depends on the agreement. Show those costs explicitly and avoid counting the same expense in both the retainer and a separate charge.

What if incidents exceed the estimate?

Use actual time records to recalibrate the plan and follow the agreed change process for out-of-scope work. Define approval for extra hours before they are needed.

Does this calculator provide market rates?

No. It calculates from your own assumptions. Its defaults are illustrative and are not based on a survey of market rates.

Summary

A sustainable maintenance retainer starts with realistic hours, a stated incident reserve, labor cost, a margin target, and clear boundaries. Track actual work and adjust the estimate based on evidence rather than assumptions that are never reviewed.
